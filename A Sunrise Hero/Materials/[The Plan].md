# 🌅 DAILY WRITING SCHEDULE (Repeat 6 days/week)

You can adjust the times, but keep the structure.

---

## 🧠 Phase 1: Warm-Up (20 minutes)

**Goal:** Enter writing mode without pressure.

- 5 min: Re-read yesterday’s writing (no editing, just read)
    
- 5 min: Write 3 bullet points about today’s scene
    
- 10 min: Free-write in the world (character thoughts, lore, dialogue snippets)
    

No perfection allowed.

---

## ✍️ Phase 2: Deep Writing Block (60–90 minutes)

**Goal: 800–1,200 words per day**

- Timer set for 25 minutes
    
- 5 minute break
    
- 25 minutes
    
- 5 minute break
    
- Optional final 25 minutes if momentum is strong
    

Rules:

- No editing
    
- No rereading
    
- No research mid-session (write [RESEARCH THIS] and continue)
    
- Phone in another room
    

---

## 🧩 Phase 3: Micro-Planning (10 minutes)

Answer:

- What happens tomorrow?
    
- What emotional shift occurs?
    
- What conflict escalates?
    

Write tomorrow’s first sentence before stopping.

This eliminates next-day resistance.

---

## 📊 Daily Target

If you write:

- 1,000 words/day
    
- 6 days/week
    

You’ll produce:

- 6,000 words/week
    
- ~24,000 words/month
    
- A 90,000-word fantasy novel in ~4 months
    

Even 800/day finishes a book this year.

|---|---|---|

Seeing accumulation kills procrastination.

You must write at least:

Momentum > motivation.

External pressure works.

# 🎯 What 1,000 Draft Words Actually Look Like

They look like:

- Slightly clunky dialogue
    
- Overwritten descriptions
    
- Repeated phrases
    
- Too much exposition
    
- Emotions stated too directly
    
- Placeholder lines like: `[describe magic better later]`
    

They are functional words.

Not final words.

---

# 🧱 Think of 1,000 Words as: Scene Expansion

Since you’re an outliner, here’s how 1,000 words usually break down:

### Example Scene Outline:

- Protagonist sneaks into archive.
    
- Guard almost catches them.
    
- They discover forbidden prophecy.
    
- Realize villain knows their name.
    

That becomes:

---

### What 1,000 Words Might Contain:

**200–300 words**  
Setting + sensory detail  
Dust, torchlight, smell of parchment, tension.

**300–400 words**  
Internal thoughts  
Fear. Doubt. Justification.

**300–400 words**  
Dialogue + action  
Footsteps. Close call. Discovery moment.

That’s it.

That’s 1,000 words.

It’s not epic prose.  
It’s expanded bullet points.

---

# 📝 Here’s What “Good Drafting” Looks Like

Instead of:

> The ancient archive loomed with oppressive grandeur, a testament to centuries of imperial ambition.

Draft version:

> The archive was bigger than she expected. The ceilings were high and the air smelled old. Too quiet. Every step echoed more than she liked.

Not beautiful.  
But usable.

---

# 🔥 The Real Secret

Draft words are scaffolding.

Revision removes:

- Repetition
    
- Clunky phrasing
    
- Over-explaining
    

Revision adds:

- Subtlety
    
- Rhythm
    
- Voice
    

Trying to do both at once is what freezes you.

---

# 💡 Your 1,000 Words Should Aim To Do ONLY 3 Things

When drafting a scene, ask:

1. What changes in this scene?
    
2. Who makes a decision?
    
3. What new problem is created?
    

If those three things happen, the words did their job.

---

# ⚖️ Quality Standard for Draft 1

If it:

- Moves plot forward
    
- Deepens character
    
- Raises tension
    

It is successful.

Even if it’s ugly.

---

# 🧠 Reframe This

Instead of:  
“I need to write beautifully.”

Say:  
“I need to write clearly enough that Future Me can revise it.”

That’s the bar.

---

# 📊 Practical Breakdown of 1,000 Words

To make it concrete:

1,000 words is roughly:

- 3–4 paperback pages
    
- 5–6 double-spaced pages
    
- About 10–15 minutes of reading time
    

It’s one solid scene chunk.

Not a masterpiece.

# 🧙 Fantasy-Specific Permission Slip

Subtlety comes later.

Clarity comes first.

# Chapter 13 – “The Minister of Steel”

### What 1,200 Draft Words Might Actually Contain

We’ll break it into chunks.

---

# 🔹 PART 1 — The Gate (250–350 words)

**Purpose:** Establish tension + power imbalance.

You’d write:

- Physical description of Tahan Estate
    
    - Iron gates? Steel insignia? Guards in uniform?
        
    - What does this say about the family?
        
- Dan’s internal state
    
    - Is he nervous? Angry? Defensive?
        
- Guards stop them.
    

Dialogue (messy draft version):

> “State your business.”
> 
> “We’re here to see Minister Iraga.”
> 
> “The Minister does not receive unannounced visitors.”

Dan reacts. Maybe he bristles.

Then Caide interrupts.

This section does not need elegance.  
It just needs:

- Tension
    
- Social hierarchy
    
- A problem (they aren’t being let in)
    

---

# 🔹 PART 2 — The Phrase (150–250 words)

**Purpose:** Show intrigue + Caide competence.

Caide pulls the steward aside.

He gives the specific phrase.

You don’t need to overcomplicate it yet.

Draft might look like:

> Caide leaned close and said something too quiet for Dan to catch.
> 
> The steward’s face changed. Just slightly.
> 
> “Wait here.”

You can even write:

> [insert coded phrase later once I decide what it is]

Totally acceptable in draft.

The gate opens.

Small victory.

---

# 🔹 PART 3 — Entering the Estate (150–200 words)

Atmosphere.

- Courtyard description
    
- Wealth signals
    
- Steel motifs everywhere?
    
- Workers forging? Silent soldiers?
    

Fantasy novels breathe here.  
But draft-level breathing.

Example draft tone:

> Everything here felt sharpened. The statues. The hedges. Even the servants moved like they were afraid of cutting themselves on something unseen.

It doesn’t have to be poetic.  
Just suggestive.

---

# 🔹 PART 4 — Negotiations Turn Sour (300–400 words)

This is the meat.

Purpose:

- Establish Tahan Iraga’s contempt
    
- Undermine Dan
    
- Raise stakes
    

Iraga enters.

What matters:

- How does he look?
    
- What’s his energy?
    
- How does he dismiss Dan?
    

Draft dialogue can be blunt:

> “You?” Iraga said, unimpressed. “You’re the one making demands?”

Let Dan try.

He explains purpose.

Iraga interrupts.  
Corrects him.  
Talks down to him.

Maybe:

- Calls him inexperienced.
    
- References political weakness.
    
- Questions his authority.
    

Dan loses control of the room.

That’s the emotional shift.

---

# 🔹 PART 5 — Caide Takes Over (250–350 words)

Purpose:

- Power reversal
    
- Shift in tone
    
- Show military authority
    

Caide speaks calmly.

He references:

- Shared campaigns
    
- Old battlefield alliances
    
- Something Iraga respects
    

The room shifts.

Draft-level directness is fine:

> “You and I both know how this ends if you refuse,” Caide said quietly. “And you know I’m not speaking politically.”

Iraga reacts differently to Caide than to Dan.

That’s key.

This section is about subtext.  
But in draft, subtext can be obvious.

---

# 🔹 PART 6 — The Challenge (200–300 words)

Talks collapse.

Iraga decides:

- Pride > negotiation.
    

Maybe he stands.

Maybe he removes a glove.

Maybe steel imagery mirrors the title.

Draft could be very straightforward:

> “If you want my cooperation,” Iraga said, “earn it.”

Challenge issued.

Dan shocked.

Caide accepts.

End chapter on:

- A line of tension
    
- Or the first clang of steel
    
- Or Dan realizing this is bigger than politics
    

That’s your hook.

Excellent question.

Fantasy writers often try to make someone dangerous by:

- Giving them long villain speeches
    
- Describing them as terrifying 12 different ways
    
- Making everyone in the room think “he was dangerous”
    
- Adding excessive physical intimidation
    

That’s overwriting.

Danger is not declared.

It is implied.

Let’s make Iraga dangerous the clean way.

---

# 🔥 Rule #1: Dangerous People Don’t Perform Danger

Weak antagonists posture.

Dangerous ones are controlled.

Instead of:

> Iraga slammed his fist against the table, fury blazing in his eyes.

Try:

> Iraga didn’t raise his voice.  
> He just stopped smiling.

Or:

> Iraga let the silence stretch longer than was comfortable.

Control = power.

---

# ⚔️ Rule #2: Make Other People Adjust Themselves

You don’t tell us he’s dangerous.

You show how others behave around him.

Examples:

- The steward speaks more formally when he enters.
    
- A guard straightens immediately.
    
- Dan suddenly becomes aware of how young he sounds.
    
- Even Caide measures his words more carefully.
    

That’s it.

If the room shifts when he enters, readers feel it.

---

# 🧊 Rule #3: Underreaction Is Scarier Than Overreaction

If Dan challenges him:

Overwritten version:

> “How dare you!” Iraga roared.

Controlled version:

> Iraga looked at Dan for a long moment.  
> “You are very confident,” he said softly.

Soft reactions imply calculation.

Calculation implies capability.

---

# 🧠 Rule #4: Give Him Leverage, Not Volume

Danger isn’t about anger.

It’s about:

- Influence
    
- Resources
    
- Reputation
    
- The ability to hurt someone without moving
    

For example:

Instead of threatening directly, he could say:

> “You’re far from home, aren’t you?”

Or:

> “It would be unfortunate if your supply routes experienced delays.”

He doesn’t say “I’ll ruin you.”

He implies it.

Readers connect the dots.

---

# 🪓 Rule #5: Specific Competence

Let him know things he shouldn’t.

Example:

Dan thinks negotiations are private.

Iraga casually references:

- A private conversation
    
- A troop movement
    
- A weakness in their plan
    

Even one line like:

> “You’re down three battalions on the southern ridge, aren’t you?”

Now he’s dangerous.

Because he’s informed.

---

# 🩸 Rule #6: One Small Act of Casual Cruelty

You don’t need brutality.

Just something precise.

Examples:

- He corrects a servant mid-sentence and the servant visibly pales.
    
- He interrupts Dan and never lets him finish a thought.
    
- He dismisses someone who clearly fears disappointing him.
    

Even this works:

> The steward began to speak.  
> Iraga didn’t look at him.  
> “Leave.”
> 
> The steward left immediately.

Power shown. Not explained.

# 🧱 Draft-Level Implementation for Your Scene

Here’s how you might subtly adjust your negotiation section.

Instead of:

> Tahan Iraga was an imposing man with a cruel expression and eyes like cold steel.

Try:

> Iraga entered without announcement.  
> No one in the room acknowledged it, but every servant stepped half a pace back.

That’s it.

No adjectives required.

---

When Dan speaks:

> Dan laid out the proposal carefully.

Iraga response:

> Iraga listened without interrupting.  
> When Dan finished, Iraga said,  
> “Is that what they told you to say?”

That line does three things:

- Undermines Dan
    
- Implies outside control
    
- Shows confidence
    

No yelling required.

---

# ⚖️ The Overwriting Test

After writing a line, ask:

Am I telling the reader he’s dangerous?  
Or am I letting them feel it?

If you used:

- “terrifying”
    
- “intimidating”
    
- “powerful”
    
- “dangerous”
    

Delete it.

Replace it with behavior.

---

# 🧊 Final Trick: Give Him Stillness

In tense moments, everyone moves except him.

People:

- Shift in chairs
    
- Tap fingers
    
- Clear throats
    

Iraga:

- Does not move.
    
- Does not rush.
    
- Does not fill silence.
    

Stillness reads as control.

Control reads as danger.
