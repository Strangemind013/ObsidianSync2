**Updated:** 27 November 2026  
**Scope:** Caide and Dan's plotline, supporting characters, magic, and chapter planning.

This is the working record of what we settled on, including ideas accepted and moved on from. **Later corrections take priority over earlier versions.** Extra suggestions from the filled chapter plans remain separate from decisions developed together.

#### 1. Dan's central conflict is his relationship with his father
Dan starts sincerely loyal. He wants to honor his father, make him successful, and earn recognition as his son. When Dan accomplishes something, his original intention is generally for **his father to receive the public credit**. Dan privately enjoys knowing he was the clever one, but he isn't initially building a rival power base.

His arc remains **Delusion → Awakening → Responsibility**: he mistakes service for a route to belonging, discovers that route never existed, and eventually takes power and responsibility for himself.

#### 2. Dan helped make his father important
His father began as a relatively ordinary noble with middling influence. Raising Dan and giving him an Academy education brought an exceptionally capable administrator into his household. Dan's knowledge, negotiations, and management helped elevate him.

We settled on **Minister of the House of Works** for his current position. He shouldn't begin as the overwhelmingly powerful head of State Affairs.

Works also gives the relationship practical connections to construction, roads, bridges, industry, and the city's emergency response.

#### 3. His father's refusal to recognize Dan is rooted in birth
His father believes nobles possess a divine right to rule. Dan is common-born, so his father never truly considers him eligible to stand beside him as a legitimate noble son.

Withholding recognition keeps Dan striving and useful. Dan's decisive realization is:

**There is nothing he could accomplish that would earn the place he wants. His father valued his service, but never intended to grant him belonging.**

This realization precedes Dan arranging his death. It makes the break larger than a quarrel over the princess.

#### 4. Dan can have personal money while being treated as a servant
We accepted the distinction between **being rewarded as a valuable employee** and **being accepted as a son**.

Dan can receive generous compensation, bonuses, and legitimate financial opportunities through his work. That explains his savings without giving him independent noble standing.

Dan interprets those rewards as evidence of paternal affection and eventual acceptance. Their limitation is that his father can pay him well, trust his competence, and depend on him while still viewing him as a subordinate.

#### 5. The grain investment connects Dan's introduction and demotion
Dan anticipates a food shortage and invests a large amount of his own money in grain and food stores. His intention is sincere: protect the city and deliver a success his father can claim.

Caide subsequently coerces him into spending much of his remaining available money on the prosthesis. After demotion, Dan also loses the income and support attached to his position.

That makes him **short of accessible money**, even though he still has wealth tied up in grain.

**Working direction:** The demotion is based on **overstepping his authority or making unauthorized commitments in his father's name**. That fits better than straightforward embezzlement when the money invested was Dan's own. The precise transaction and accusation still need choosing.

#### 6. Dan's introduction should demonstrate what later gets punished
We moved toward introducing him through the grain problem: he identifies a danger, negotiates around obstruction, uses his father's delegated authority, and expects the resulting success to strengthen their relationship.

His introduction should establish his competence, affection for his father, precarious status, and private relationship with the princess.

The useful contrast with the later “Dan solves problems” chapter is that **early Dan can operate through his father's authority; demoted Dan must discover what he can accomplish after that authority is withdrawn**.

**Still open:** The exact opening negotiation.

#### 7. The firefighting assignment is an impossible punishment
His father removes Dan from his influential representative role and relegates him to stopping the summer fires—something he expects Dan to fail at.

Ordinary volunteers are unwilling to oppose what they regard as the Summer God's seasonal work. Dan therefore recruits prisoners and persuades Caide to lead them.

We specifically removed an additional complication: **the prisoners are not being actively hunted or threatened with immediate return to prison**. Dan has arranged sufficient cover through the warden and relevant authorities. Their eventual pardons remain important, but we don't need a fugitive subplot.

#### 8. The firebreak is an emergency response
Their first attempt with water fails. Caide then develops the idea of destroying the next suitable house to prevent the fire spreading.

When a fire happens, the crew arrives, identifies the necessary house, and acts. They aren't selecting all the sacrificial houses beforehand.

Their first success therefore creates two consequences at once: they save a larger area, and they create understandable anger among people whose homes they destroyed.

**Chapter sequence:** Caide discovers the idea in the preceding chapter, presents it during Dan's chapter, and executes it in the following firefighting chapter.

#### 9. Dan makes Caide's idea possible
We settled on three main jobs for Dan's practical chapter:

- **Religious toleration:** Argue that they are interfering with houses rather than extinguishing the Summer God's fire.
- **Compensation:** Pressure officials or merchants into supporting households whose homes must be destroyed.
- **Equipment:** Obtain tools, supplies, and protective coats.

The compensation developed beyond simply promising money: rebuilding materials and labor can be useful, but **a displaced family also needs somewhere to sleep immediately**.

A promising negotiation involved Dan threatening to expose a merchant's irregularities, only for the merchant to point out Dan's own involvement or signatures. That gives Dan resistance instead of an effortless blackmail victory.

His father receiving public credit remains consistent with Dan's loyalty at this stage.

#### 10. Caide's early betrayal is his own choice
We decided against the ledger-theft/blackmailer subplot.

Caide arranges the dangerous situation that lets him coerce Dan into funding his prosthesis. He reveals his usefulness as a Sage and exploits Dan's need for protection.

The important change is accountability: **Caide chooses to betray his friend because he wants the arm badly enough**. Someone else isn't forcing him into it.

That also gives his subsequent apology a clearer purpose.

#### 11. The prosthesis represents more than physical convenience
It promises relief from everyday difficulties, but also a return to the fighting style and identity Caide values.

He was an exceptional swordsman using a long and short blade, balanced with sagecraft. Losing an arm disrupted that balance. Recovering it appears to mean recovering himself.

The established sequence is:

1. Dan finances the prosthesis.
2. Caide orders the rare jade it requires.
3. Grin burns the ship carrying it, and it sinks.
4. Caide loses hope.
5. A later opportunity allows him to obtain the jade and arm.

The major twist remains: **the prosthesis blocks his sagecraft, and he initially doesn't know this**.

Eventually he must surrender it permanently. It cannot simply be reattached or replaced afterward.

#### 12. Caide's growth concerns dependence and acceptance
His arc remains **Self-Doubt → Heroism → Acceptance**.

He believes his injury makes him useless and explains why he was abandoned. His need to prove otherwise can get in the way of team success.

What he eventually learns is that his worth and heroism survived the injury, and that receiving help doesn't invalidate them.

**His decisive sacrifice comes near the end, when he gives up the arm.** We don't need to force the earlier jade-versus-pardons moment into an equally final renunciation.

#### 13. The prisoners need bonds and indispensable contributions
The training chapter should focus on the prisoners' developing relationship with Caide, rather than becoming a generic montage of increasingly impressive spells.

Some discover Sage potential; others don't.

- **Batch** shares Caide's destructive affinity and initially uses it through his axe.
- **Harum** is the large, gentle man developing protective magic.
- A non-Sage constructs the small cart-mounted ballista used to restrain an opponent while Sol's companions position seals.

The non-Sages should remain essential participants.

We also wanted each prisoner to receive a POV chapter where possible. **Sweet-tooth's recruitment chapter and Harum's training chapter have clear places.** The later assignments for the other prisoners in the filled chapter plans were suggestions, rather than individually approved decisions.

#### 14. Sweet-tooth's home subplot is being cut
The short prison extraction and woods recruitment sequence stays, but the later home visit and family material could become another subplot the book couldn't afford.

The working cut is **before they go to Sweet-tooth's home**, retaining the recruitment sequence and its perspective on the crew.

The overall target remains approximately **120,000 words**. That has repeatedly favored combining functions within existing chapters rather than adding separate storylines.

We also settled on **Harum**, replacing Pong/Pung. Harun was considered along the way.

#### 15. Sol and Tahan use the same Hiyang differently
Both are attuned to **Judgement**.

**Tahan** directs it toward himself, augmenting his physical abilities into an extraordinary martial force. He earned **the Demon of Red Valley** title by defending a chokepoint against an army. He recognizes something of himself in Caide.

Excessive magic use sends Tahan into a rage. **Sol's established role is containing his father**, so Sol already possesses substantial expertise.

Sol suppresses enemies through increased gravity over an area. His three companions prepare the chain of seals that makes his restraint possible.

He also uses **Destiny to nudge metal studs on their clothing**, communicating direction and coded instructions. This is coordination, not merely another offensive spell.

#### 16. Grin's capture contrasts with the Faceless fight
Against Grin, the practiced plan goes wrong in places. The crew improvises, everyone contributes, and they nevertheless capture him.

But **Grin wanted to be captured**. Their achievement is real without proving they could have forced an unwilling Grin into lasting captivity.

Later, against the Faceless, the team performs the maneuver properly: ballista, positioning, seals, suppression.

That later success pays off the training and shows their growth. The new problem is the enemy's abilities, rather than the crew simply repeating its earlier mistakes.

#### 17. The jade choice is an apparent betrayal
Caide chooses the jade instead of securing immediate pardons. The reader should fear he has abandoned the prisoners, then discover that he still intends to help them.

Crucially:

- He **doesn't already have a concrete alternative pardon plan**.
- The engagement is announced **after** he chooses the jade.
- He later uses the staged rescue to obtain the pardons.

We explored him believing that two hands would make him better able to handle future dangers and fulfill his obligations. We also considered his wounded pride after needing everyone to defeat Grin.

**Still open:** The exact balance of those motivations. We did **not** settle a mandatory “the jade expires unless taken immediately” explanation.

#### 18. The wedding crisis delays the marriage and obtains pardons
The princess loves Dan. Their relationship is secret, and his standing is insufficient to marry her, while his father's is sufficient.

Dan incites riots during the wedding procession. Caide removes her from the palanquin, stages the circumstances of a rescue, and gives her a mild wound to delay the wedding.

The king and others believe Caide saved her. Caide requests the prisoners' pardons as his reward.

The delay lasts through recovery and the relevant seasonal restriction, allowing Grin's execution to occur before the marriage.

**Latest clarification:** The princess is not in on the plan. Earlier, she was said to have agreed to the injury. Those two details still need reconciling precisely; the earlier agreement should not silently be treated as settled.

#### 19. Pain and despair serve different functions for Grin
**Pain powers his magic. Despair is the currency he needs to revive his demigod lover.**

After capture, the authorities torture him and steadily increase his power. By the relevant point, Dan and Caide know he will escape and cannot prevent it.

Dan chooses to benefit from that inevitable escape. He gives Grin information about how to devastate the city in return for killing:

- His father.
- **Tahan**, the king's uncle and Meroun's greatest heroic protector.

Tahan's death is particularly valuable because it destroys public hope and removes the city's strongest figure.

Caide knowing escape is inevitable does **not automatically mean he knows about Dan's assassination bargain**.

#### 20. Dan uses a forged heir declaration for succession
We refined the document from generic “adoption papers” into something establishing or legitimizing him as his father's heir.

Dan forged it earlier as a contingency against his father dying while genuine recognition was still being withheld.

After Cardinale's death, influential nobles accept it without close investigation because they want continuity during the crisis.

The useful distinction is that **recognition as heir enables the political transition; it doesn't automatically make a government office hereditary**. The other nobles support his installation.

Dan's ending gives him agency and responsibility, while leaving the moral weight of how he obtained power intact.

#### 21. The magic system has firm rules and clarifications
Practitioners are **Sages**, more specifically Sixword Sages. A final universal name for the magic itself was not firmly settled.

| Word | Established function |
| --- | --- |
| **Judgement** | Augments or instructs properties and behavior; can also modify other magic. |
| **Warmth** | Alters temperature, including removing heat. |
| **Light** | Creates and alters light. |
| **Break** | Destroys, cracks, severs, or shatters. |
| **Endure** | Preserves an existing state. |
| **Destiny** | Directs an object's movement. |

Spells require physical inscriptions. Their legibility, precision, and stability affect performance. Skilled Sages can combine up to three Words, with sharply increasing difficulty.

Words cannot directly affect another living being; the caster can affect themselves.

**Hiyang** is natural attunement: usually one Word, exceptionally two, and all six in a generational Sage. Other Words remain learnable at reduced effectiveness.

Caide has **Break and Endure**. They cannot be combined in the same spell. His sword edge uses **a physical line of Break**, rather than Judgement making its physical edge sharper.

Grin is the manifestation of **Warmth**—the correction replaced the earlier “Heat.”

Seyla's Hiyang is **Light**. Her signature beam combines **Light, Break, and Destiny**.

#### 22. The coats receive secret magical assistance
The firm idea is that **Caide secretly improves their protection through sagecraft**, while the crew remains unaware he is a Sage at that stage.

We explored historical-style materials and construction to translate modern protective functions into the setting. The original notes used leather; layered quilted cotton was another direction.

**Still open:** The exact material and enchantment. We did not finalize a manufacturing recipe.

#### 23. Personal planning is separate from the proposed outlines
The chapter process starts with rough structure before drafting prose: purpose, goal, resistance, causal actions, turning point, outcome, continuity, scenes, and closing beat.

We created the reusable template and explanatory guide.

More recently, the decision was to fill the plans personally. There are now **32 blank templates under Book 1 → Caide & Dan Plotline [Planning]**, named from the original notes, with only chapter titles filled in at creation.

The preferred formatting is compact fields, **`####` headings**, and **no blank line immediately beneath headings**.

The filled notes under Writing Help remain a reference containing proposals. Their specific replacement titles, prisoner POV assignments, word budgets, dragon response, and other connective inventions should not be mistaken for personally approved decisions.
