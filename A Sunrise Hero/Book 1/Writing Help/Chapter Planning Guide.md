Use [[Chapter Template]] to turn a rough chapter idea into a sequence you can draft. Copy the template into a chapter note, then answer its core fields in one or two sentences each. Expand the difficult parts as needed.

The essential preparation is understanding why the chapter exists, what the character pursues, what interferes, how the situation turns, and what changes afterward. Exact dialogue, gestures, incidental discoveries, and some staging can emerge during drafting.

# Planning approach

This template combines several published craft approaches:

- **Scene and sequel:** Randy Ingermanson explains pursuit through goal, conflict, and setback, followed by reaction, dilemma, and decision. This helps connect events through character responses. [Writing the Perfect Scene](https://www.advancedfictionwriting.com/articles/writing-the-perfect-scene/)
- **Story Grid:** The Five Commandments connect an inciting incident, progressive complications and a turning point, crisis, climax, and resolution. These help identify the pressure that leads to a consequential response. [The Five Commandments of Storytelling](https://storygrid.com/articles/five-commandments-of-storytelling)
- **Scene questions:** K. M. Weiland uses a specific question and its answer to identify a scene's throughline. [A New Way to Think About Scene Structure](https://www.helpingwritersbecomeauthors.com/scene-structure/)
- **Chapter pacing:** Writing Excuses discusses chapters as tools for pacing, emphasis, and transitions. A chapter may contain several scenes or interrupt a continuing scene. [Pacing With Chapters](https://writingexcuses.com/12-15-pacing-with-chapters/)

The template is a practical synthesis. Use its fields to clarify the story rather than forcing every chapter to have the same rhythm. The neutral label “outcome” allows for victories, failures, discoveries, and unresolved problems.

# Chapter details

**Chapter number and working title:** Use a descriptive title that helps locate the event. The final published chapter need not retain it.

**POV:** Identify whose experience and interpretation shape the chapter. A witness viewpoint can work, but give that person a reason to care. Harum can experience training through his own uncertainty and desire to help, even while Caide leads the session.

**Time and location:** Establish where the chapter belongs in the timeline and which places it uses. Note a meaningful constraint such as nightfall or an approaching procession.

**Essential characters:** List the people whose actions, needs, or presence matter. Additional background figures can emerge during drafting.

**Approximate word budget:** Use this to protect space within the book. It is an estimate, not a quota to fill.

# Purpose

**Main contribution to the book:** State the chapter's principal job from the author's perspective. It might establish a relationship, complicate an objective, expose a limitation, provide a payoff, or change the balance of power.

**What would be lost if this chapter were removed:** Identify the missing cause, experience, or understanding. This helps distinguish a necessary chapter from information that could fit elsewhere.

Keep author purpose separate from character goal. “Establish Dan's dependence on his father” is an author purpose. “Secure backing for the firebreak crew” is Dan's immediate goal. A strong chapter can achieve both through the same events.

# Character motivation

**Immediate goal:** What concrete result does the POV character want during the chapter? “Obtain compensation funds” gives the character something to pursue. “Develop as a leader” describes a broader arc.

**Underlying motive:** Why does this result matter personally? Dan can sincerely want to protect Meroun and honour his father while hoping to earn recognition as a son.

**Stakes:** What could the character lose, damage, or fail to obtain? Include immediate consequences. The crew might be unable to operate, a promise might become impossible to keep, or someone's trust might deteriorate.

Useful stakes make the character's tactics matter. They need not always involve physical danger.

# Dramatic question

**Question driving the chapter:** Frame the pursuit as a question a reader can follow. For Dan's Chapter 8: “Can he obtain the backing and resources for Caide's plan after losing authority?”

**Answer or meaningful progress provided:** Identify the chapter's contribution toward an answer. A complete answer is useful when the chapter finishes the relevant action. A chapter break can also leave the decision pending or carry the action into another chapter.

An abstract thematic question such as “What makes a good leader?” may inform the story. The dramatic question should connect to what is happening now.

# Opposition and tension

**External resistance:** Identify what specifically obstructs the goal: a religious objection, a rival claim, missing equipment, dangerous conditions, or an opponent's action.

**What the opposing character wants:** Give that person an objective. A merchant might want to protect his accounts, avoid liability, and preserve a profitable relationship. These interests make negotiation more specific than simple hostility.

**Internal pressure or competing desire:** What makes responding difficult for the POV character? Dan wants to honour his father but may need to commit his name without explicit approval. Caide wants to protect his crew while also proving that he remains exceptional.

Resistance should affect the pursuit. General unpleasantness or an argument unrelated to the goal will not provide the same forward movement.

# Causal sequence

**Starting situation:** Record the conditions the character enters with, including relevant consequences from the previous chapter.

**Trigger:** What makes action or reconsideration necessary now? The problem may already be underway when the chapter opens.

**First approach:** What does the character try, and why do they expect it to work?

**Complication:** What frustrates or changes that attempt? A useful complication develops from the situation or someone else's interests.

**Response:** How does the character adjust? This response should help cause the next development.

**Turning point:** What action or discovery makes the current approach insufficient? It might expose a mistaken assumption, remove an option, introduce decisive information, or force a different tactic.

Expand this section into roughly five to eight event bullets as a starting point. Add or remove beats according to the chapter. Check that the sequence can be explained through causes and responses rather than a succession of unrelated incidents.

# Decision and action

**Options the character perceives:** What do they believe they can do after the turning point? Their understanding may be incomplete or mistaken.

**Cost or risk of each option:** What makes the response difficult? Costs can involve reputation, money, loyalty, uncertainty, exposure, or responsibility.

**Decision:** Which course do they choose?

**Action taken:** How does the choice become visible? A decision to trust someone matters when the character lets that person carry out an essential task.

**If the chapter breaks before completion:** Record where the pending response continues. This prevents a pacing choice from becoming a forgotten thread.

The choice can be modest. A conversation, discovery, or planning chapter does not require a spectacular moral crisis. It does require enough development to justify the attention given to it.

# Outcome and change

**Practical result:** What succeeds, fails, or remains unresolved?

**Before to after:** Describe a meaningful shift: unsupported to provisionally backed, confident to uncertain, mistrusted to relied upon, free of obligation to personally committed.

**Character interpretation:** What does the character believe the outcome means? That interpretation can differ from what the reader understands.

**Relationship effect:** Does trust, affection, dependence, resentment, or authority change?

**Next consequence:** What does the outcome make possible or necessary afterward?

A chapter need not move a character closer to the truth. Dan can act competently and become more convinced that achievement will earn his father's recognition. The practical situation changes while his mistaken belief is strengthened.

Leave room for reaction where it matters. A quiet scene in which a character processes a loss and forms a new intention can be an important causal link.

# Reader knowledge and continuity

**What the reader must understand:** Identify information needed to follow the action, evaluate the stakes, or appreciate a later payoff.

**What the POV character knows or misunderstands:** Keep knowledge consistent with the chosen viewpoint.

**What remains concealed and why the concealment is fair:** Record intended uncertainty or misdirection. An outside observer can misinterpret Caide's jade decision. A viewpoint character who already knows his reasoning should not internally pretend ignorance merely to mislead the reader.

**Earlier setup paid off:** Which promise, object, relationship, signal, or rule gains significance here?

**Later event prepared:** What must be established for a future development to feel earned? Sol's retreat signal in training can prepare its use in the final battle.

**Relevant worldbuilding or magic rules:** Note only the rules that affect the chapter's choices and outcome. Resolve a major uncertainty if the planned climax depends on it.

# Scene list

For each scene, record:

**Location — immediate objective — resistance — outcome**

A chapter can have one scene or several. Each additional scene should contribute something distinct. A negotiation might produce the practical result, while a short private conversation reveals what the protagonist thinks that result means.

A montage can use brief attempts and reactions across time. Identify the development that makes those moments accumulate into a meaningful chapter.

# Closing beat

Choose the final action, exchange, image, discovery, or unresolved development that deserves emphasis.

A closing beat can offer relief, unease, anticipation, grief, or a new intention. It does not always need a cliffhanger. Consider what the reader will carry into the next chapter and whether this is a useful place to pause.

The exact last line can emerge during drafting.

# One sentence summary

Because **[situation]**, **[character]** tries to **[goal]**, but **[opposition]** forces them to **[response]**, resulting in **[change or consequence]**.

If this sentence remains vague, identify the missing action or causal connection. It is a diagnostic, not prose intended for the novel.

# Drafting readiness

Begin with clear answers to **purpose, goal, underlying motive, opposition, turning point, and outcome**. One or two sentences per field is sufficient for a first pass.

Then check:

- The resistance interferes with the goal.
- The character's attempts and responses connect causally.
- The intended outcome changes or advances something meaningful.
- Essential viewpoint knowledge, timing, and rules are settled.
- The chapter can be summarised through actions and consequences.

You can begin drafting with smaller details unresolved. Exact dialogue, gestures, incidental discoveries, minor staging, and the precise closing image can remain open. If a central choice has no credible motive, or the ending relies on an undefined rule, resolve that part first.

# Dan Chapter 8 example

This example uses the version discussed in the outline conversation and remains open to revision.

- **Purpose:** Demonstrate Dan's leadership while showing how he frames success as service to his father.
- **POV:** Dan.
- **Immediate goal:** Obtain the backing and resources that let Caide attempt the firebreak method.
- **Underlying motive:** Protect Meroun, honour his father, and earn renewed approval.
- **Dramatic question:** Can Dan get the crew operating after losing much of his former authority?
- **Opposition:** Religious condemnation and a merchant who knows Dan's authority has weakened.
- **First approach:** Use familiar pressure and knowledge of the merchant's dealings.
- **Turning point:** The merchant counters by pointing to Dan's own involvement in the questionable transactions.
- **Decision and action:** Dan negotiates a contribution that provides practical help, preserves reputations, and gives his father public credit.
- **Practical result:** The crew obtains backing, equipment, and compensation arrangements.
- **Human consequence:** Dan insists that displaced families receive immediate accommodation as well as rebuilding materials.
- **Before to after:** An unsupported plan becomes provisionally workable; Dan gains obligations.
- **Character interpretation:** He believes the achievement should bring him closer to recognition as a son.
- **Later payoff:** The crew can execute the firebreak in the next chapter, and the equipment supports later encounters.

A possible scene division is a short exchange with Caide, a religious negotiation, and a merchant negotiation that includes compensation and equipment. The chapter ends before the actual firefighting.
